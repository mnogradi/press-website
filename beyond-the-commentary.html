Beyond the Commentary: The Lesser-Known Works of John Gill

By Morgan Nogradi

November 3, 2026 • 3 min read

John Gill (1697–1771) is best known for his nine-volume Exposition of the Old and New Testaments. But if you think roughly 7,500 pages of commentary ought to be enough Gill for anyone, think again. His other works include historical investigations, literary criticism, controversial theology, and some rather unexpected subjects.

Take funeral sermons. In An Essay on the Original of Funeral Sermons, Orations, and Odes (1729), Gill traces the custom from ancient Egypt through Greece and Rome, including a humorous reference to women employed to sing “doleful ditties” at funerals. Eventually, he arrives at the sermons of his own day, and has a few things to say about them. One, he declares, contains “neither law nor gospel, good learning, nor good sense.”

Of another preacher, who had published both a funeral sermon and an accompanying ode, Gill offers a wonderfully sarcastic appraisal:

“I confess, Sir, I have but little judgment in poetry, yet I'm ready to conclude, 'tis the best of these his performances.”

Gill also complains of the extravagance of funeral elegies, wishing the characters portrayed were “more just.” Apparently, he was not easily impressed.

He was equally unimpressed by the philosophical fashion of grounding morality in “the nature and fitness of things.” In The Moral Nature and Fitness of Things Considered (1738), Gill takes aim at a sermon by Samuel Chandler and its underlying assumptions. The title sounds genteel enough; the argument is considerably sharper.

Then there’s singing. In A Discourse on Singing of Psalms as a Part of Divine Worship (1734), Gill defends congregational psalmody and considers what singing actually is. A harmonious, melodious sound of the voice, certainly. But the tuneful notes of birds? And what about the grunting of a hog? Gill has definite opinions on the subject.

Or perhaps you’d prefer 282 pages on Hebrew. His Dissertation Concerning the Antiquity of the Hebrew Language, Letters, Vowel-Points, and Accents (1767) is exactly what it sounds like. Some men have hobbies.

One of Gill’s most revealing works, however, was written near the end of his life. In Dying Thoughts (published posthumously in 1773), he begins by examining (apologies to Charles Spurgeon, who deplored long negative divisions) what readiness for death is not, methodically clearing away false foundations before explaining what it is.

And then there are the sermons themselves: volumes of them, on subjects both familiar and decidedly unexpected. One example is Christ the Saviour from the Tempest, preached in commemoration of the catastrophic Great Storm of 1703.

The Exposition is only the beginning. And for those looking to venture beyond it, there is no better place to start than his final reflections.
